Most budgeting apps start by asking for your bank login. That works for some people, but it has two problems. Many people don't want to hand an app ongoing access to their bank. And even when they do, the bank only knows the total of each shop: "REWE €48.20" or "Tesco £36.15". It can't tell you whether that money went on milk, coffee or nappies.

If what you want to know is **what you spend on food and everyday items**, the receipt is the better source. It lists every item, its price and any discount. Here are three ways to use it, from the most manual to the most automatic.

## 1. A notebook or spreadsheet

The simplest method: keep your receipts for a month, then enter them.

1. Put every receipt in one place: an envelope, a drawer, a photo album on your phone.
2. Once a week, enter each one in a spreadsheet with four columns: **date, store, category, amount**. If you want to track specific items, add a row per item instead of per receipt.
3. At the end of the month, add up each category.

**Good for:** a one-off check of where your money goes. **Downsides:** it takes 10–20 minutes a week, and most people stop after a few weeks. Item-level tracking in a spreadsheet is slow, because a weekly shop can have 30 lines.

## 2. A budgeting method without bank sync

Envelope-style budgeting apps let you enter spending by hand without connecting a bank. You decide on a grocery budget for the month and log each shop against it.

**Good for:** staying under a fixed amount. **Downsides:** you still type in every purchase, and you track totals per shop, not what you bought.

## 3. Scan your receipts

A receipt-scanning app reads the receipt for you. You take a photo and the app turns it into data: store, date, every item, the price and the category. Because it works from the receipt, it sees exactly what you bought, and it never needs your bank.

When choosing one, check:

- **Does it read every item, or only the total?** Many receipt apps are built for business expenses and only capture the total, date and merchant.
- **Where does the reading happen?** Some apps upload the photo to a server to read it, others read it on your phone. See our guide to [receipt scanner privacy](/en/guides/receipt-scanner-privacy-cloud-vs-on-device/).
- **Does it need an account?** If you don't want your data stored by someone else, look for an app that works without signing up.
- **Can it handle your receipts?** Long supermarket receipts, weighed items, discounts and receipts in other languages or currencies.

## Make it a habit

Whichever method you choose, the hard part is keeping it up. A few things help:

- **Scan or note receipts the same day**, before they fade or get thrown away. Thermal receipts can become unreadable within months.
- **Ask for the receipt** even when the cashier doesn't offer one. Many stores also offer digital receipts in their app or by email; those can be saved as PDFs.
- **Look at the numbers once a week.** Five minutes on a Sunday shows you more than a full review at the end of the year.
- **Track one or two items you care about,** such as coffee, meat or baby food. Seeing the price of one item over time is often more motivating than a category total.

## What to look for after a month

After four weeks you'll have enough data to spot patterns:

- **Your real weekly grocery spend,** including the small top-up shops that are easy to forget.
- **The items you buy most often** and what they cost you per month.
- **Price differences between stores** for the same item.
- **Categories that grew,** such as snacks, drinks or ready meals.

That's usually where the savings are: not in cutting everything, but in a few items you buy often and pay more for than you thought.
